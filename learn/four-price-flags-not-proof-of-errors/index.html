Four price flags, not proof of errors

November 20, 2026 · October 2026 whole-file re-scan

We re-scanned 66 large-hospital price files whole-file, 52.9M rate rows in all. Four patterns recur. Each one is a flag that tells a reviewer where to look, not proof of what they will find.

How we checked

Files re-scanned whole-file
66 large-hospital price files
Rate rows checked
52.9M
Errors established by this scan
None

Across these 66 files, the scan did not establish a single error in the categories tested. That is not the same as declaring the files error-free: deciding whether a flag is a real mistake takes the underlying contract and the source system, which a file scan cannot see. What the scan produced is a set of recurring patterns that deserve a second look. A file can show several flags, so the counts below overlap.

The four flags

Flag 1 · A rate far above its own median

One payer's negotiated rate sits at twenty times or more the file's median for the same billing code. Sixty of the 66 hospitals show at least one.

Often innocent: a niche service with a real carve-out rate, or a median dragged low by many small rows. Worth a review when the outlier sits on a high-volume code, or when the contract team cannot produce the rate behind it.

Flag 2 · One rate, many payers

The same dollar amount appears for four or more payers on a single item. Forty hospitals show this.

Often innocent: one parent company prices several plans the same way, or the payers share a fee schedule. Worth a review when unrelated payers repeat the same rate across many codes, which suggests a copied column rather than negotiated rates.

Flag 3 · Negotiated above list price

A negotiated dollar amount lands above the hospital's own gross charge for the item. Thirty-five hospitals show this.

Often innocent: the charge master simply sits below some contract rates for that item. Worth a review at scale, because a pattern of negotiated rates above list price undercuts the assumption that negotiated means discounted.

Flag 4 · Penny rates with no unit

Most $0.01 rates in these files are drug rows that carry a unit, consistent with per-unit pricing; those are not flags. The flag is a $0.01 rate with no drug unit, seen at 56 hospitals.

Often innocent: a placeholder habit from a template. Worth a review because a penny with no unit tells a reader nothing; if it marks a rate as unknown, the file should say so.

How to review a flag

  1. Pull the full row with its payer and plan context, not just the flagged field.
  2. Check whether the pattern repeats across codes and payers, or stands alone.
  3. Compare the row against the underlying contract terms before concluding anything.
  4. Ask the data owner to trace the value to its source system.
  5. Record the explanation. Do not edit the file simply to clear a warning.

What this does not establish

A flag starts a review. It does not end one.

These counts describe the 66 files we re-scanned, not all hospitals. A flag is not proof that a price is wrong, that a vendor caused it, or that CMS has found a violation. Hospital, payer and vendor names are withheld.
