What our price files tell us

YieldGuard · November 20, 2026

A 16.2% paired cash-price gap, a volume-ranked reimbursement queue, and the controls worth fixing first. The 25 original questions, with measured findings and operating decisions.

01 · Compliance + CFO

Before our executive signs the price-file attestation, what can we verify, and what still needs internal evidence?

Treat attestation as a release decision, not a validator receipt.

Require a signed evidence bundle with the exact file hash, required-field and cross-field results, identity checks, and the hospital owner's approval of the contract and remittance inputs. Our sweep's 179 flagged files out of 1,282 shows why a readable file is not enough. A file-only review can clear publication controls; it cannot attest that the underlying contracts and claims were supplied completely. The practical call is to separate those two approvals and stop a release when either owner has not signed.

02 · Compliance + revenue integrity

Our file passes the CMS validator. What important problems can still be hiding in it?

Passing a parser does not make the contents credible.

Our 1,282-file sweep found 183 semantic findings across 179 files, or 14.0% of the files reviewed. The dominant issue was a zero remittance count alongside a claim-derived allowed-amount statistic: 137 findings, covering 25.0 million affected published rows. Another 50 findings concern missing or improper explanations, with four findings in both groups. Put consistency checks after format validation. That is where this evidence says the bigger control gap sits.

04 · CFO + compliance + procurement

How do we independently check that our price-transparency vendor delivered what we paid for?

Buy a reproducible acceptance test, not a vendor's assurance.

Make delivery acceptance depend on a saved copy of the file and a rerunnable result. Reject count-zero rows that encode allowed-amount percentiles; require the appropriate explanation for no remittances; verify that the delivered URL serves the reviewed bytes; and reconcile declared facilities and payer-plan inventory with hospital-owned lists. Route extreme prices and unusual cross-payer patterns to review instead of automatically rejecting them. Our register contains 104 findings on one platform's hosts, enough to show why checking the delivered output is necessary even when file production is outsourced.

05 · Compliance + revenue integrity

Which zero-count rows with populated allowed amounts need review, and which apparent gaps are actually permitted?

Fix zero-count statistics before debating price outliers.

A zero remittance count and a populated median, p10 or p90 cannot both describe the required historical calculation. CMS says not to encode those statistics when the count is zero. In the 137 inverse-encoding findings, the median affected-row count is 60,916 and the median affected share is 81% of applicable rows. Resolve the count from the source remittances, then either calculate the statistics or omit them and supply the required no-remittance explanation. A missing statistic on a properly explained zero-count row is not the same problem.

06 · Compliance + finance operations

What changed in our published file since the last approved version?

Use a semantic change log, not just a new filename.

Compare the released file with the previous approved bytes in four layers: facility identity, payer-plan coverage, service inventory, and charge/statistic changes. Report additions, removals and changed values separately, then tie each material change to an approval. A changed hash only proves the bytes changed; an unchanged hash proves no byte change. Our isolated 48-hour identical-file check demonstrates that second test, but the retained material does not contain a complete approved-version history from which to state a real hospital's release-by-release changes.

07 · Compliance + CFO

Which price-file issues should our team fix first?

Work the queue in this order: contradictory statistics, missing explanations, then price anomalies.

The first tier has the clearest rule and the largest measured row exposure: 137 inverse-encoding findings and 25.0 million affected published rows. The second tier is explanation completeness: 50 findings, four overlapping the first tier. The third tier is targeted price review. A 20x-median value, an identical amount across several payers, or a charge above gross can be real. Assign a source owner and a due date to tiers one and two; require a clinical/unit/contract check before changing tier-three prices. This is our recommended triage order, not a measured estimate of repair effort.

08 · Compliance + system finance

Does our MRF cover the right campuses, locations and organizational NPIs?

Start the identity review with the exceptions, not all 257 headers.

The existing header review found 236 files declaring an organizational NPI. Registry checks leave 23 of those files with no declared NPI name match, and two with state mismatches under the any-NPI rule. Those are the first identity-review queue. Separately, the stricter every-declared-NPI rule matches 205 of 236 files, which catches mixed system lists. Registry name matching checks whether the declarations are plausible; a hospital-owned campus and NPI roster is the control that settles whether anything was omitted. Do not merge those two tests.

10 · Compliance + IT

How often do publicly accessible files become stale, unreachable or materially different without anyone noticing?

The first stale-file queue is 29 files, not the whole directory.

Among 256 parseable declared update dates in the 257-file header cohort, 29 are older than 365 days as of November 15, 2026. That is 11.3% of that dated cohort. Start refresh checks there. The median declared age is 228 days; the remaining age bands are 52 within 90 days, 34 at 91-180 days and 141 at 181-365 days. The directory separately records 3,782 price-transparency links and 2,394 direct files among 5,421 hospitals. These are two different control views: declared age versus discoverability. Neither snapshot measures an outage or unnoticed-change frequency.

11 · Revenue integrity + compliance

Are our 10th percentile, median, 90th percentile and counts reproducible from the required remittance data?

A remittance audit is the proof. A price-file consistency check is the first gate.

Rebuild a sample from the remittance population used for the file, using the same service/package and payer-plan scope, a 12-15 month lookback, and the CMS treatment of zero-dollar remittances and percentile rounding. Reconcile the population count before comparing the three percentiles. The zero-count screen already identifies a concrete population to investigate: 137 findings in our sweep. The retained files do not include the remittance records, so no numerical claim that their percentiles reproduce is made. Our release decision is to withhold a reproducibility sign-off until that source-to-file trace exists.

13 · Compliance + CFO

How do our file's specific review flags compare with similar hospitals?

Use the large-hospital cohort as a review-pattern reference, not a pass mark.

In the 66-file deep re-scan, 60 files had a 20x-or-greater median outlier, 56 had a one-cent row without a drug unit, 40 had identical amounts across at least four payers, and 35 had negotiated amounts above gross. These screens are common enough that their presence alone is a poor way to rank a hospital's control quality. Compare affected share and cause within a matched service and hospital group instead. The current evidence supports a strong operational conclusion: contradictory remittance statistics deserve priority over a raw anomaly count.

19 · Compliance + vendor management

Do corrections stay fixed after the next vendor release?

A fix is complete only when it survives the next release.

Turn each accepted correction into a regression assertion: the affected row/key, the expected relationship, its owner and the accepted file hash. Run that assertion on the next vendor delivery alongside the semantic diff. Reopen the issue if the bad relationship returns, even if the filename and declared date changed. We have an isolated identical-file snapshot check, but no retained correction-to-next-release series. The control can be specified now; an observed recurrence rate cannot be calculated from this material.

20 · Compliance operations + CFO

How long do specific issues take to resolve, and where does remediation stall?

Plan remediation in weeks; do not use CMS's closure clock as your fix target.

In the disclosed closed-case universe, warned cases reaching a closure notice took a median 99 days, with the middle half taking 68-152 days (3,234 cases). That is an enforcement-process interval, not the hospital's hands-on repair time. Our recommended operating rule is to assign the source owner immediately, accept or reject a proposed correction on a defined internal deadline, and measure days from first detection to a verified corrected file. If a ticket lacks an owner, source evidence or a verified replacement hash, the process has stalled regardless of whether CMS has written again.

21 · Compliance + counsel + CFO

What does CMS actually act on, and what happens after a warning or corrective-action request?

CMS's disclosed enforcement record is dominated by notices and resolution, not fines.

The dataset contains 13,355 activity records across 8,038 cases. Among 3,646 cases with a warning notice, 1,098 also received a corrective-action-plan request, 3,234 have a closure notice and 14 received a penalty notice. That is 30.1%, 88.7% and 0.4% of warned cases respectively; the paths overlap. The business conclusion is to build a repeatable response-and-correction process, not sell a penalty scare. The published case universe is closed-case based, and action types do not identify the underlying substantive violation, so these are not open-case risk probabilities.

22 · CFO + procurement

Is maintaining this in-house cheaper than buying a vendor and independent checks?

Own the controls even if a vendor produces the file.

Our recommended starting architecture is outsourced file production plus hospital-owned acceptance tests and an independent spot check. The repeated patterns in delivered files make vendor assurance alone a weak control, but that is not an argument to rebuild every formatting and hosting function in-house. The cost decision is a break-even calculation: compare the vendor plus independent-check cost with loaded internal build, maintenance, hosting and response time. No current vendor quotes or hospital staffing inputs are in this evidence, so the pack makes an operating-model recommendation rather than inventing a cheaper dollar option.

23 · Patient financial services + compliance

Does our patient estimate or shoppable display describe the same service scope as the file?

Most of the public display needs a catalog join, not more fuzzy matching.

The accessible display in the two-hospital pilot lists 630 services. Only 30 expose a billing code; all 30 occur in the retained MRF by code. The other 600 display items are plain-language labels. Strict normalized name matching reconciles 213 of those 600, leaving 387 that need the display's internal catalog mapping. Prioritize that mapping rather than claiming 387 missing services from vocabulary differences. The second display required login and was not enumerated. The useful conclusion is that code-level presence can be tested directly; patient-facing scope needs the hospital's display-to-service catalog key.

24 · CFO + strategy

How do our published pricing patterns look alongside our financial condition and peer hospitals?

The current financial signal is a cohort-selection warning, not a distress detector.

The 24 name-and-location-confirmed exploratory matches have FY2024 median net patient revenue of 38.9 million versus 115.9 million in the full financial cohort; their median patient-care operating margin is 12.1% versus 14.1%. But 27 of the 38 provisionally matched findings come from one platform's hosts. That concentration is a strong reason not to attribute the observed financial difference to price-file quality. The actionable next comparison is within matched size, service and hosting groups, using the 24 resolved records as the starting pool. A hosting choice alone does not establish who caused a finding.

25 · CFO + compliance + audit committee

What should our board see each quarter to know price-transparency controls are working?

Give the board a control scorecard, not a catalog of unusual prices.

Lead with four measures: reviewed files/releases, unresolved rule-based findings, the share of affected applicable rows, and corrections that remain fixed at the next release. Our current research baseline can populate the first two: 1,282 files reviewed, 179 files with findings and 183 findings, including 137 inverse-encoding findings. The 25.0 million affected rows are an exposure count, not lost revenue. For a hospital's quarterly board pack, add owner and age to each unresolved item and show verified closures separately from vendor promises. Recheck durability is the next operating measure to collect, not a number to fabricate.
